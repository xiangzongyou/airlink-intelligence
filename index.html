Degree counts direct destinations; betweenness measures how often an airport is the bridge on the shortest trip between two others. They disagree most for gateway airports: Anchorage has the highest betweenness in the country with only 34 destinations. Click a row to see it on the map.

The communities

Small nationally, severe regionally

For single-airport closures: the share of one region’s airports that get cut off (colored bar) next to the share of all US trips lost (gray bar). Regions are communities with at least 20 airports. Click a bar to see that airport on the map.

Caveats. Tiny communities (3–12 airports) are left out of the regional measure, because losing one of three airports would read as 33%. Airline shares are slightly inflated by codeshare records, and small-carrier names are unreliable in OpenFlights. Alaska results inherit known gaps such as the missing Unalaska–Anchorage route.

Targeted closures break the network apart far faster than random ones. In the full network, closing Anchorage and then Seattle cuts Alaska off, which is why betweenness targeting looks so damaging; in the Lower 48 alone, degree and betweenness targeting perform about the same.

Source: OpenFlights routes.dat (snapshot of June 2014), with airports from airports.dat plus airports-extended.dat for airports missing from it. Scheduled passenger routes only: no cargo carriers, no flight frequencies, no passenger counts.

Cleaning: nonstop domestic routes; airline duplicates and codeshares merged into one link per airport pair (codeshare records are kept, since about 22% of pairs appear only as codeshares). Sightseeing tours (Boulder City–Grand Canyon West, Grand Canyon–Page) and one cargo-only leg are excluded. The result: 605 airports and 2,883 routes.

Definitions: a trip is any pair of open airports that could reach each other before a closure. A trip is lost if no itinerary remains, and detoured if the shortest itinerary needs more connecting flights. An airport is cut off if it is no longer in the largest connected part of the network. Route importance is edge betweenness: the share of shortest trips that use the route.

Known gaps: the Unalaska (DUT)–Anchorage route is missing, so the Dutch Harbor group and the Boeing Field seaplane group look disconnected from the start; closures never count them as “cut off.” Results for remote airports should be checked against BTS T-100 data before being reported.
